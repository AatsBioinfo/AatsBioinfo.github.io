Who brings the histones to the DNA? And what we still don't know

Histone chaperones, from a 1978 frog-egg protein to 2024 structures of the replication fork, and six open questions about chromatin for new students.

By Aathma Merin Bejoy Bioinformatician, Berlin 12 Nov 2026 ~5 min read

In Part 1 I looked at how DNA wraps around histones, and in Part 2 at the chemical tags on the histone tails. One question I skipped: how does a histone get onto the DNA in the first place?

This last part is about the proteins that carry histones around, the histone chaperones. It ends with the things about chromatin that are still not understood, in case you are a student looking for a question to work on.

01 · HISTONE CHAPERONESWho brings the histones to the DNA?

In Part 1 I covered what makes histones and DNA stick. But a strongly positive protein floating around a nucleus full of negative DNA would stick to the wrong things. Something has to carry it and put it down in the right place. These are the histone chaperones.

The idea goes back to 1978, when Ron Laskey's group showed that nucleosomes are assembled by an acidic protein, nucleoplasmin, which binds histones and transfers them to DNA.1 The title of that paper is basically the definition of a chaperone. Being acidic (negative) lets a chaperone hold the positive histones safely until the right moment.

In my notes I wrote that FACT is "the most important" one. That was too strong. What makes FACT stand out is that it can handle both H2A–H2B and H3–H4, and it works exactly where nucleosomes have to be taken apart and put back: in front of RNA polymerase and at the replication fork.

02 · OPEN QUESTIONSWhat is still unclear (and where a new student could start)

Writing this made me realise how much of the "basics" is still being argued about. If you are starting a master's or PhD and looking for a question, these are the ones that kept coming up in my reading.

Do histone tails hold the nucleosome together, or help take it apart?

Lorch & Kornberg say the tails' positive charge adds instability;7 the 2026 nanopore preprint says the tails act as clamps that make unwinding harder.8 These used very different methods. Both may be true in different contexts, such as with or without a remodeller, or in a single nucleosome versus an array.

Start with: refs 7, 13, 14 and 8 side by side · PDB 1AOI for the structure

Does chromatin really phase-separate inside living cells?

Nucleosomes clearly form droplets in a test tube,9,4 but an influential 2019 perspective argued that the evidence for phase separation inside cells is often phenomenological and cannot tell it apart from other mechanisms, and that its function in vivo is even less clear.10 Most of the new 2025–2026 condensate papers in this post are still in vitro or in simulation.

Start with: ref 10 for how to test it properly, then refs 9 and 4

What does chromatin look like above the nucleosome, in real cells?

Imaging inside human cells did not find the textbook 30-nm fibre: in 2017, ChromEMT saw disordered chains 5–24 nm wide,11 and the 2026 in-cell cryo-ET study saw irregular, partly stacked heterochromatin.12 How this structure differs between cell types, and between active and silent regions, is still open.

Start with: refs 11 and 12 · look up cryo-electron tomography

Are histone marks a cause or a consequence?

For most marks we know where they sit, not what they do. H3K4me3 was only shown in 2023 to have a direct role, and it was a different role (pause release) from the one most people assumed (initiation).13 The same question is open for many other marks, and even more so for the newer acylations.14

Start with: ref 13 for how to design the experiment (acute removal, not slow knockouts)

How are marks inherited when the cell divides?

We now have structures of FACT and Mcm2 holding parental histones at the fork,6 but how faithfully the marks are restored on both new DNA strands, and how this goes wrong in disease, is still being worked out.

Start with: ref 6 · search "parental histone recycling"

For computational people: what do we do with single-cell, multi-mark data?

Methods now measure six or more marks, or marks plus DNA methylation, in the same single cell.15–19 The 2015 Roadmap paper had to impute and combine marks across bulk samples.20 How to model combinations of marks per cell, at sparse coverage, is a very open analysis problem.

Start with: the Roadmap chromatin-state models (ChromHMM) · the public data in refs 15–18

WRAPPING UPWhat I take away

This series started with a simple question: how does two metres of DNA fit into a six-micrometre nucleus? The first answer, histones, has been solid for fifty years. But the reason they stick, the role of their tails, and what the packed DNA looks like inside a real cell are all still being rewritten, some of it this year.

For my own work, the biggest change is how I look at a ChIP-seq track. A peak of H3K4me3 is not a label stuck on a gene. It is a chemical group on a floppy, charged tail that can grab DNA, neighbours and other proteins, and the cell keeps putting it on and taking it off.

Next I want to go one level up: loops, TADs and how the genome is folded in 3D. If you spotted a mistake here, please tell me. I am re-learning, and I would rather be corrected than stay wrong.
