FASTA, FASTQ and the code hidden in your read files

The first two file types anyone meets in sequencing, what each line means, and how a single character like I or 5 stores the quality of a base.

By Aathma Merin Bejoy Bioinformatician, Berlin 6 Aug 2026 ~7 min read

When you start in bioinformatics, two file types show up almost immediately: FASTA and FASTQ. At first they look like walls of A, C, G and T with random punctuation in between. Once you know what each line does, they are simple. This post goes through both, and then through the part that confuses almost everyone: the quality scores.

In this post

  1. FASTA: sequence only
  2. FASTQ: four lines per read
  3. What a quality score means
  4. Phred+33: a number as one character
  5. What you see in real files today

01 · FASTASequence only

FASTA stores a biological sequence (DNA, RNA or protein) and almost nothing else. A record has two parts:

The first line always starts with >. That is the header: a name for the sequence plus any description. A FASTA file can hold one sequence or millions, each with its own header. It is the right format when you only care about what the sequence is, for example a reference genome you align your reads to.

FASTA has one blind spot. A sequencer is not equally sure about every base it reads. Some it reads clearly, others are close to a guess, and FASTA has no place to record that. That is the gap FASTQ fills.

02 · FASTQFour lines per read

FASTQ stores the same sequence letters plus a quality score for every base. It started at the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and was used for years without a formal definition, in several incompatible variants, until a 2010 paper wrote down the standard.1 It is what comes out of Illumina sequencers, and where most analyses begin. A FASTQ record is always four lines:

The one rule to remember: line 2 and line 4 have the same length. Every base has exactly one quality character under it.

03 · QUALITY SCORESWhat a quality score means

Each character on line 4 encodes a Phred quality score, a number for how likely the base call is to be wrong. The idea comes from the base-calling program phred, written for Sanger sequencing in the 1990s by Brent Ewing and Phil Green. It estimated an error probability for every base call, and those estimates matched the real error rates.2 Illumina still assigns its quality scores with a phred-like algorithm.3

Q = −10 × log10(Perror)

In words: every time the error probability drops ten-fold, Q goes up by 10. A base with a 1 in 1,000 chance of being wrong has Q30.2

QChance the base is wrongAccuracy
101 in 1090%
201 in 10099%
301 in 1,00099.9%
401 in 10,00099.99%

Q30 is the number people quote for "good quality". Illumina reports that the large majority of bases from its instruments score Q30 or higher.3

04 · PHRED+33A number as one character

If the scores are numbers like 30 and 40, why does line 4 look like IIIII?5+!? Because of the one-character-per-base rule. "30" is two characters and would break the alignment with the sequence, so each score has to fit into a single character.

The trick is ASCII, the table that gives every keyboard character a number. In FASTQ files, a quality score is stored as the character whose ASCII code equals the score plus 33.4 That is why the encoding is called Phred+33. The +33 skips the codes below 33, which are invisible control characters such as tabs and line breaks, so the first usable score, Q0, becomes !.

ASCII code of the character = Q + 33

Decoding the example string by hand:

  • I → 73 − 33 = Q40
  • ? → 63 − 33 = Q30
  • 5 → 53 − 33 = Q20
  • + → 43 − 33 = Q10
  • ! → 33 − 33 = Q0

Watch out for 5. It means Q20, not five. It is an ASCII character that happens to look like a digit, and this mix-up catches most people at first.

One more trap: older Solexa and Illumina pipelines used other variants, including an offset of 64 instead of 33.1 If an old dataset has quality strings full of lowercase letters like h and g, check which encoding it uses before trimming or aligning.

05 · REAL FILESWhat you see in real files today

If you open a FASTQ from a recent Illumina run, you will notice something odd: the quality line uses only three or four different characters. That is not a bug. Quality scores take up a large share of the storage space of sequencing data, and Illumina found that they can be compressed into a few bins without affecting alignment or variant calling.5

▸ LATEST · QUALITY SCORES2023–2026
  • Illumina · 2023NovaSeq X software v1.2Three bins, and Q40 at the top. The NovaSeq X groups its quality scores into three bins. From control software v1.2 on, all scores from 3 to 17 are reported as Q12, 18 to 29 as Q24, and 30 and above as Q40.6 With the XLEAP-SBS chemistry the top bin moved from Q37 to Q40, and the middle bin from Q20 to Q24.7 So in Phred+33 a NovaSeq X quality line uses mainly - (Q12), 9 (Q24) and I (Q40). Try the "NovaSeq X-style" button in Figure 3.
  • Illumina · 2023Q-score calibrationHow good are the scores themselves? Illumina reports that the NovaSeq X can produce 85% or more of its output bases with a Q40 score.7 Note that this is the manufacturer's own figure.

For everyday work this means two things. A quality line full of I on a new instrument is normal and not suspicious. And any tool that plots per-base quality will show steps instead of smooth curves, because there are only a few possible values.

WRAPPING UPThe whole thing in three lines

FASTA stores sequences and nothing else. FASTQ adds a quality score for every base, in four lines per read. Phred+33 turns each score into one printable character by adding 33 and looking it up in the ASCII table.

Almost every sequencing analysis I run, from quality checks to alignment, starts with these files, so this is where I started the blog too.
