Re-learning biology · Part 1

Two metres of DNA in a six-micrometre nucleus. How does it fit?

I went back to the basics of DNA packing and found that one of the first things I learned, "positive histones stick to negative DNA", is only half the story.

By Aathma Merin Bejoy Bioinformatician, Berlin 12 Nov 2026 ~15 min read

I work with sequencing data every day. ChIP-seq peaks, read pileups, coverage tracks. At some point I realised I was very comfortable with the files and much less comfortable with the biology that makes them. So I started re-learning the basics from the beginning, with a notebook and the original papers, and this blog is where I write it up.

Part 1 is about the very first problem a cell has: how to pack its DNA. For each topic I start with the textbook picture, then add what papers from the last few years (2022 to 2026) say about it. At the end there is a list of things that are still not understood, in case you are a student looking for a question to work on.

01 · THE PACKING PROBLEMThe numbers that don't seem to fit

DNA is written in four letters, A, T, G and C, and we count its length in base pairs (bp). The first truly complete human genome sequence, finished by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium in 2022, is 3.055 billion base pairs long.1 That is one copy. Most of our cells carry two copies, one from each parent, spread over 46 chromosomes.

If you took all the DNA out of one human cell and laid it end to end, it would be about two metres long. It has to fit inside a nucleus that is only about six micrometres across.2,3 The DNA molecule itself is very thin, only about 2 nanometres wide,4 so the problem is not its volume. The problem is length: it has to be folded without getting tangled, and still be readable when a gene is needed.

The textbook answer to "how does it fit?" is a hierarchy: DNA wraps around proteins to make beads, the beads fold into a fibre, the fibre folds into loops, and so on. The first step of that hierarchy is very well understood. The higher steps, as we will see, have been redrawn more than once.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · PACKING2022–2026
  • Science · 2022Nurk et al., T2T consortiumThe genome is finally complete. The T2T-CHM13 sequence filled in the last ~8% of the human genome that the old reference left unfinished, mostly repetitive heterochromatin, which is exactly the tightly packed DNA this post is about.1
  • Nature Communications · 2026Kreysing, Cruz-León, Betz et al. (Beck & Hummer labs)Looking at nucleosomes inside real human cells. Using cryo-electron tomography on primary human T cells, this team mapped individual nucleosomes at the edge of the nucleus. The heterochromatin there is tightly packed but irregular. They did not see the classic, ordered 30-nanometre fibre from textbooks, but a looser structure about 37 nm wide with some stacked parts and some messy parts.5

02 · THE NUCLEOSOMEBeads on a string

The first level of packing is the nucleosome. In 1974 Roger Kornberg, then a postdoc at the MRC in Cambridge working with Aaron Klug and Francis Crick, proposed that chromatin is built from a repeating unit of histone proteins and DNA.6,7 (I had written "Stanford" in my notes, but the 1974 model was proposed while he was at the MRC.)

Today we know the core of the nucleosome very precisely. In 1997 Karolin Luger and Tim Richmond's group solved its crystal structure: two copies each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 form an octamer, and 145–147 bp of DNA wrap around it.8 The DNA between two nucleosomes is called linker DNA, and linker histone H1 sits where the DNA enters and leaves.

The crystal structure also showed something that matters later: the amino-terminal tails of the histones pass over and between the turns of DNA and reach out to contact neighbouring particles.8 So the tails are not part of the tidy core. They hang out into the surroundings.

03 · THE CHARGE STORYWhy do histones and DNA stick together?

This is the explanation I learned first. Histones carry an exceptionally high positive charge, mostly concentrated in their tails,9 because they are rich in the amino acids lysine and arginine. In fact, essentially all proteins that condense DNA are rich in lysine, arginine or both.10 DNA, on the other hand, is negative. Opposites attract, so the DNA wraps tightly around the histones.

Why is DNA negative? Every nucleotide is joined to the next by a phosphate group in the sugar–phosphate backbone. Phosphate comes from phosphoric acid (H3PO4), and acids give away hydrogen ions (H+). At the pH inside a cell, around 7, the oxygens of the phosphate give up their protons and become negatively charged.11 The backbone phosphates have a pKa of about 2,12 far below 7, so in a living cell they are essentially always charged.

So the picture seems complete: a positive protein ball, a negative DNA string, electrostatic attraction, done. That is what I believed until I went looking for more recent papers.

04 · THE TWISTCharge is not the main glue

The surprising part

In 2023 the Kornberg lab itself, the lab that proposed the nucleosome, wrote that charge–charge interactions contribute little to the stability of the core nucleosome. Instead, hydrogen bonding and nonpolar interactions play the major role, and the octamer's very high affinity for DNA comes from having many such contacts.9

In the same paper, Yahli Lorch and Roger Kornberg turned the usual idea of the tails around. The common belief is that the positive tails help stabilise the nucleosome by neutralising the DNA's negative charge. They found the opposite: the high positive charge contributes to instability. The tails are needed for the remodelling complex RSC to remove the histone octamer, and for the octamer to transfer from a nucleosome to free DNA, and this activity comes from their positive charge.9 In other words, the positive tails may be there partly to help the cell take nucleosomes apart.

This does not mean charge is unimportant. Several groups published on exactly this question in 2025 and 2026, and they study different things: the tails, the core, single nucleosomes, or whole arrays. Together they give a more mixed picture, which I think is the honest one.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · TAILS & CHARGE2025–2026
  • Communications Biology · Feb 2026Hu, Zhang, Xu, Liu & PengSimulations with Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. 81 microseconds of all-atom molecular dynamics. When the divalent ions were added, contacts between the histone core and DNA stayed mostly the same, but tail–DNA contacts dropped significantly, and the tails (especially H3) became more mobile.13 This fits the idea that the tails are the "loose" part.
  • Nature Communications · Sep 2026Li … PanchenkoTails do compact chromatin. Combining simulations with cryo-ET of native human chromatin, this study found three ways the tails promote compaction: tail–DNA contacts, H2A–H4 tail–tail contacts and H3–H4 tail–tail contacts. H3 tails reach across linker DNA to neighbouring nucleosomes, offset their DNA's negative charge and pull them closer.14 So between nucleosomes, charge clearly matters.
  • Nucleic Acids Research · Feb 2026Hammonds, Singh, Suresh, Yang et al.Which positive residues matter. Changing the pattern of basic residues in the H3 tail changed whether nucleosomes form "condensates" (droplets) and how viscous they are. The number, identity and position of the basic residues all mattered.15
  • Preprint · Mar 2026 · not peer reviewedResearch SquareTails as "electrostatic clamps". Pulling DNA off single nucleosomes through a nanopore: removing the tails significantly lowered the voltage needed to unwind the DNA, but the unwinding pathway stayed the same.16 This leans against "tails destabilise", though with a very different method from Lorch & Kornberg.
  • Nature · 2025"Native nucleosomes intrinsically encode genome organization principles"Charge and 3D genome. Acetylation on histone tails affected nucleosome condensation much more than acetylation on the core, with the strongest effect on the H4 tail.17

My reading of all this: inside the core, the many specific contacts do most of the holding. The charged tails act more like adjustable hands, loose on their own nucleosome, but able to grab DNA, neighbours, or each other. That is also where the cell puts most of its chemical tags, which brings me to the next part.

05 · HISTONE MARKSChemical tags on the tails

Names like H3K9me3 or H3K27ac are everywhere in my work. They are chemical groups that the cell's own enzymes attach to histones after the protein is made, which is why they are called post-translational modifications (PTMs). The famous ones are acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitination. Since around 2011, mass spectrometry has added many more: lactylation, crotonylation, succinylation, citrullination, propionylation, butyrylation and others. A 2024 review counts nine of these "novel" types alone.18 Lactylation was found on histones in 2019, with 28 sites on the core histones in the first study.19

Roadmap Epigenomics did for the epigenome something like what the Human Genome Project did for the sequence. It published 111 reference human epigenomes in 2015, profiled for histone modifications, DNA accessibility, DNA methylation and RNA expression.21 One thing that took me a moment: there is one human reference genome, but there is no single reference epigenome. The epigenome is the full collection of chemical tags and proteins on the DNA and its histones, and it is different in every cell and tissue type. That is why they needed 111.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · HISTONE MARKS2023–2026
  • Nature · 2023Wang, Fan … HelinDoes H3K4me3 actually do anything? For years people argued whether H3K4me3 just marks active promoters or helps make them active. By removing it acutely in mouse embryonic stem cells, this study showed it is not needed for transcription to start, but without it RNA polymerase II pauses more and elongates more slowly. H3K4me3 recruits a protein (INTS11) that helps release the paused polymerase.22
  • eLife · 2026H3K115acMarks on the core, not only the tails.   • Signal Transduct. Target. Ther. · 2024Yao, Hu & Wang (review)The new acylations. A good overview of nine newer histone modifications (lactylation, citrullination, crotonylation, succinylation and others) and what is known about their links to disease.18

06 · METHODSHow we read the tags, and the open DNA

This is the part where the biology turns into the files I work with. There are two different questions. Where is a particular mark? That is ChIP-seq, and newer methods like CUT&Tag. Where is the DNA open? That is ATAC-seq.

ChIP-seq (chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing) uses an antibody against, for example, H3K4me3, pulls down the DNA fragments carrying it, and sequences them. It works, but it suffers from low signal, high background, epitope masking from cross-linking, and it needs a lot of cells.24

CUT&Tag (2019, Henikoff lab) keeps the antibody but turns the process inside out: the antibody binds in the cell and brings along a protein A–Tn5 transposase fusion, which cuts the DNA right there and inserts sequencing adapters. The result is high resolution and very low background, from small numbers of cells and even single cells.24

ATAC-seq (2013, Greenleaf and Chang labs) uses no antibody at all. Hyperactive Tn5 transposase inserts sequencing adapters into native chromatin wherever it can reach, which means wherever the DNA is open. It needs only 500 to 50,000 cells and also tells you about nucleosome positions and transcription factor binding.25 As someone who works in a transposon lab, I love that the key enzyme in ATAC-seq is a bacterial transposase.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · METHODS2025
  • Nature · 2025TACIT / CoTACITSingle-cell histone marks in early embryos. TACIT gives genome-coverage single-cell profiles of seven histone modifications across mouse early embryos, from zygote to blastocyst.26 Its extension CoTACIT maps up to six marks in the same cell.27
  • Nature Methods · Sep 2025Geisenberger et al., scEpi²-seqHistone marks and DNA methylation on the same molecule. Combines sortChIC with TAPS conversion to measure histone modifications and DNA methylation at the same time in single cells.28
  • Science Advances · 2025scMTR-seqSix marks plus RNA. Profiles six histone modifications and the transcriptome in the same single cells.29
  • Nature Communications · Dec 2025sc-hPTMNo antibodies at all. A mass-spectrometry method that identifies and quantifies histone modifications in single cells across H1, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, without choosing the marks in advance.30

07 · HISTONE CHAPERONESWho brings the histones to the DNA?

So far I covered what makes histones and DNA stick. But a strongly positive protein floating around a nucleus full of negative DNA would stick to the wrong things. Something has to carry it and put it down in the right place. These are the histone chaperones.

The idea goes back to 1978, when Ron Laskey's group showed that nucleosomes are assembled by an acidic protein, nucleoplasmin, which binds histones and transfers them to DNA.31 The title of that paper is basically the definition of a chaperone. Being acidic (negative) lets a chaperone hold the positive histones safely until the right moment.

In my notes I wrote that FACT is "the most important" one. That was too strong. What makes FACT stand out is that it can handle both H2A–H2B and H3–H4, and it works exactly where nucleosomes have to be taken apart and put back: in front of RNA polymerase and at the replication fork.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · CHAPERONES2022–2025
  • Nature · Mar 2024Li, Gao, Zhang … ZhaiParental histones caught at the replication fork. Cryo-EM structures of the yeast replisome show FACT at the front, grabbing parental histones from the DNA ahead, together with the histone-binding domain of Mcm2.35 This is how old histones, and the marks on them, can be passed to the two new DNA copies.
  • Science · 2022RNAPII elongation complex with FACTWatching a nucleosome come apart and go back together. Cryo-EM structures of RNA polymerase II passing through a nucleosome with FACT show how the nucleosome is disassembled ahead of the polymerase and reassembled behind it.34
  • Nature Communications · 2025Gao et al.A chaperone that changes chromatin droplets. H4 tail lysines drive phase separation of nucleosome arrays, while NAP1 and H3 acetylation make the condensed chromatin more fluid.36 A nice link back to section 4.

08 · PUTTING IT TOGETHEROpen and closed chromatin

Here is how all of this connects to genes being on or off. When nucleosomes are packed tightly over a gene's promoter, the machinery that reads the gene cannot get in. Kornberg and Lorch showed in 1987 that a nucleosome on a promoter prevents transcription from starting, which made the nucleosome a general gene repressor.37 When the nucleosomes move or loosen, transcription factors can bind, and the gene can be read.

09 · OPEN QUESTIONSWhat is still unclear (and where a new student could start)

Writing this made me realise how much of the "basics" is still being argued about. If you are starting a master's or PhD and looking for a question, these are the ones that kept coming up in my reading.

Do histone tails hold the nucleosome together, or help take it apart?

Lorch & Kornberg say the tails' positive charge adds instability;9 the 2026 nanopore preprint says the tails act as clamps that make unwinding harder.16 These used very different methods. Both may be true in different contexts, such as with or without a remodeller, or in a single nucleosome versus an array.

Start with: refs 9, 13, 14 and 16 side by side · PDB 1AOI for the structure

Does chromatin really phase-separate inside living cells?

Nucleosomes clearly form droplets in a test tube,15,36 but an influential 2019 perspective argued that the evidence for phase separation inside cells is often phenomenological and cannot tell it apart from other mechanisms, and that its function in vivo is even less clear.38 Most of the new 2025–2026 condensate papers in this post are still in vitro or in simulation.

Start with: ref 38 for how to test it properly, then refs 15 and 36

What does chromatin look like above the nucleosome, in real cells?

Imaging inside human cells did not find the textbook 30-nm fibre: in 2017, ChromEMT saw disordered chains 5–24 nm wide,39 and the 2026 in-cell cryo-ET study saw irregular, partly stacked heterochromatin.5 How this structure differs between cell types, and between active and silent regions, is still open.

Start with: refs 39 and 5 · look up cryo-electron tomography

Are histone marks a cause or a consequence?

For most marks we know where they sit, not what they do. H3K4me3 was only shown in 2023 to have a direct role, and it was a different role (pause release) from the one most people assumed (initiation).22 The same question is open for many other marks, and even more so for the newer acylations.18

Start with: ref 22 for how to design the experiment (acute removal, not slow knockouts)

How are marks inherited when the cell divides?

We now have structures of FACT and Mcm2 holding parental histones at the fork,35 but how faithfully the marks are restored on both new DNA strands, and how this goes wrong in disease, is still being worked out.

Start with: ref 35 · search "parental histone recycling"

For computational people: what do we do with single-cell, multi-mark data?

Methods now measure six or more marks, or marks plus DNA methylation, in the same single cell.26–30 The 2015 Roadmap paper had to impute and combine marks across bulk samples.21 How to model combinations of marks per cell, at sparse coverage, is a very open analysis problem.

Start with: the Roadmap chromatin-state models (ChromHMM) · the public data in refs 26–29

WRAPPING UPWhat I take away

I started with a simple question: how does two metres of DNA fit into a six-micrometre nucleus? The first answer, histones, has been solid for fifty years. But the reason they stick, the role of their tails, and what the packed DNA looks like inside a real cell are all still being rewritten, some of it this year.

For my own work, the biggest change is how I look at a ChIP-seq track. A peak of H3K4me3 is not a label stuck on a gene. It is a chemical group on a floppy, charged tail that can grab DNA, neighbours and other proteins, and the cell keeps putting it on and taking it off.

In part 2 I want to go one level up: loops, TADs and how the genome is folded in 3D. If you spotted a mistake here, please tell me. I am re-learning, and I would rather be corrected than stay wrong.
