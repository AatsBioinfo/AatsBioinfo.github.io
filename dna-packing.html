Re-learning biology · Part 1

Two metres of DNA in a six-micrometre nucleus. How does it fit?

I went back to the basics of DNA packing and found that one of the first things I learned, "positive histones stick to negative DNA", is only half the story.

By Aathma Merin Bejoy Bioinformatician, Berlin 12 Nov 2026 ~8 min read

I work with sequencing data every day. ChIP-seq peaks, read pileups, coverage tracks. At some point I realised that much of the biology behind them I had learned from textbooks that are already out of date. Some of what I was taught as fact has since been corrected, and some of it is still being argued about.

So I am going back to the basics, one topic at a time. For each one I start with what the textbook says, then look at what papers from the last few years (2022 to 2026) have found, and what is still unknown. Every claim links to its source.

I start with the very first problem a cell has: how to pack its DNA. It turned into three posts. This first one is about the packing itself and what holds DNA on the histones.

01 · THE PACKING PROBLEMThe numbers that don't seem to fit

DNA is written in four letters, A, T, G and C, and we count its length in base pairs (bp). The first truly complete human genome sequence, finished by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium in 2022, is 3.055 billion base pairs long.1 That is one copy. Most of our cells carry two copies, one from each parent, spread over 46 chromosomes.

If you took all the DNA out of one human cell and laid it end to end, it would be about two metres long. It has to fit inside a nucleus that is only about six micrometres across.2,3 The DNA molecule itself is very thin, only about 2 nanometres wide,4 so the problem is not its volume. The problem is length: it has to be folded without getting tangled, and still be readable when a gene is needed.

The textbook answer to "how does it fit?" is a hierarchy: DNA wraps around proteins to make beads, the beads fold into a fibre, the fibre folds into loops, and so on. The first step of that hierarchy is very well understood. The higher steps, as we will see, have been redrawn more than once.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · PACKING2022–2026
  • Science · 2022Nurk et al., T2T consortiumThe genome is finally complete. The T2T-CHM13 sequence filled in the last ~8% of the human genome that the old reference left unfinished, mostly repetitive heterochromatin, which is exactly the tightly packed DNA this post is about.1
  • Nature Communications · 2026Kreysing, Cruz-León, Betz et al. (Beck & Hummer labs)Looking at nucleosomes inside real human cells. Using cryo-electron tomography on primary human T cells, this team mapped individual nucleosomes at the edge of the nucleus. The heterochromatin there is tightly packed but irregular. They did not see the classic, ordered 30-nanometre fibre from textbooks, but a looser structure about 37 nm wide with some stacked parts and some messy parts.5

02 · THE NUCLEOSOMEBeads on a string

The first level of packing is the nucleosome. In 1974 Roger Kornberg, then a postdoc at the MRC in Cambridge working with Aaron Klug and Francis Crick, proposed that chromatin is built from a repeating unit of histone proteins and DNA.6,7

Today we know the core of the nucleosome very precisely. In 1997 Karolin Luger and Tim Richmond's group solved its crystal structure: two copies each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 form an octamer, and 145–147 bp of DNA wrap around it.8 The DNA between two nucleosomes is called linker DNA, and linker histone H1 sits where the DNA enters and leaves.

The crystal structure also showed something that matters later: the amino-terminal tails of the histones pass over and between the turns of DNA and reach out to contact neighbouring particles.8 So the tails are not part of the tidy core. They hang out into the surroundings.

03 · THE CHARGE STORYWhy do histones and DNA stick together?

This is the explanation I learned first. Histones carry an exceptionally high positive charge, mostly concentrated in their tails,9 because they are rich in the amino acids lysine and arginine. In fact, essentially all proteins that condense DNA are rich in lysine, arginine or both.10 DNA, on the other hand, is negative. Opposites attract, so the DNA wraps tightly around the histones.

Why is DNA negative? Every nucleotide is joined to the next by a phosphate group in the sugar–phosphate backbone. Phosphate comes from phosphoric acid (H3PO4), and acids give away hydrogen ions (H+). At the pH inside a cell, around 7, the oxygens of the phosphate give up their protons and become negatively charged.11 The backbone phosphates have a pKa of about 2,12 far below 7, so in a living cell they are essentially always charged.

So the picture seems complete: a positive protein ball, a negative DNA string, electrostatic attraction, done. That is what I believed until I went looking for more recent papers.

04 · THE TWISTCharge is not the main glue

The surprising part

In 2023 the Kornberg lab itself, the lab that proposed the nucleosome, wrote that charge–charge interactions contribute little to the stability of the core nucleosome. Instead, hydrogen bonding and nonpolar interactions play the major role, and the octamer's very high affinity for DNA comes from having many such contacts.9

In the same paper, Yahli Lorch and Roger Kornberg turned the usual idea of the tails around. The common belief is that the positive tails help stabilise the nucleosome by neutralising the DNA's negative charge. They found the opposite: the high positive charge contributes to instability. The tails are needed for the remodelling complex RSC to remove the histone octamer, and for the octamer to transfer from a nucleosome to free DNA, and this activity comes from their positive charge.9 In other words, the positive tails may be there partly to help the cell take nucleosomes apart.

This does not mean charge is unimportant. Several groups published on exactly this question in 2025 and 2026, and they study different things: the tails, the core, single nucleosomes, or whole arrays. Together they give a more mixed picture, which I think is the honest one.

▸ LATEST FINDINGS · TAILS & CHARGE2025–2026
  • Communications Biology · Feb 2026Hu, Zhang, Xu, Liu & PengSimulations with Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. 81 microseconds of all-atom molecular dynamics. When the divalent ions were added, contacts between the histone core and DNA stayed mostly the same, but tail–DNA contacts dropped significantly, and the tails (especially H3) became more mobile.13 This fits the idea that the tails are the "loose" part.
  • Nature Communications · Sep 2026Li … PanchenkoTails do compact chromatin. Combining simulations with cryo-ET of native human chromatin, this study found three ways the tails promote compaction: tail–DNA contacts, H2A–H4 tail–tail contacts and H3–H4 tail–tail contacts. H3 tails reach across linker DNA to neighbouring nucleosomes, offset their DNA's negative charge and pull them closer.14 So between nucleosomes, charge clearly matters.
  • Nucleic Acids Research · Feb 2026Hammonds, Singh, Suresh, Yang et al.Which positive residues matter. Changing the pattern of basic residues in the H3 tail changed whether nucleosomes form "condensates" (droplets) and how viscous they are. The number, identity and position of the basic residues all mattered.15
  • Preprint · Mar 2026 · not peer reviewedResearch SquareTails as "electrostatic clamps". Pulling DNA off single nucleosomes through a nanopore: removing the tails significantly lowered the voltage needed to unwind the DNA, but the unwinding pathway stayed the same.16 This leans against "tails destabilise", though with a very different method from Lorch & Kornberg.
  • Nature · 2025"Native nucleosomes intrinsically encode genome organization principles"Charge and 3D genome. Acetylation on histone tails affected nucleosome condensation much more than acetylation on the core, with the strongest effect on the H4 tail.17

My reading of all this: inside the core, the many specific contacts do most of the holding. The charged tails act more like adjustable hands, loose on their own nucleosome, but able to grab DNA, neighbours, or each other. That is also where the cell puts most of its chemical tags, which is where Part 2 starts.
